# First-Draft Writing Workflow

The **first-draft writing workflow** is Nicolas Cole's source-described six-step method for turning a blank page into a finished draft by separating idea capture, reader/angle clarification, outlining, filling, and editing. Its governing constraint is to remove perfection pressure: a first draft is a rough block of marble, not a polished statue. Cole uses Michelangelo's gradual shaping of “David” as the analogy and says the writer's job is to pour disjointed or raw thoughts onto the page before refining them. This is a source-described writing method, not a verified performance law. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-turn-a-blank-page-into-a-finished-draft-in--2081358806408106390.md]

## What the first draft is for

The first draft is **only** for getting ideas onto the page. Cole rejects the assumption that a writer must start with the first word and continue linearly to the end; writing is iterative and assembled piece by piece. Trying to complete all five drafting passes in one sitting is presented as a source-described recipe for unnecessary time and frustration. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-turn-a-blank-page-into-a-finished-draft-in--2081358806408106390.md]

The five-pass sequence is:

1. **Draft 1:** Ask, “What am I trying to say to WHO? Why? And what are the big ideas?”
2. **Draft 2:** Add what is missing and say everything that needs to be said.
3. **Draft 3:** Eliminate what does not need to be said.
4. **Draft 4:** Find faster ways to say what is being said.
5. **Draft 5:** Refine, clarify, and become more specific.

^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-turn-a-blank-page-into-a-finished-draft-in--2081358806408106390.md]

Draft 1 is an outline of the main points. Cole's “freight train” instruction is to write bullet points for everything the writer wants to say, including random or seemingly unimportant concepts, without worrying about sentences, spelling, grammar, or structure. The source says to “rip out your backspace button,” continue until there is nothing left to say and the itch has stopped, and put the backspace button back only when it is time to edit. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-turn-a-blank-page-into-a-finished-draft-in--2081358806408106390.md]

## Six steps from blank page to publish

### 1. Find the “North Star”

Before drafting, define exactly who the target reader is, what the writer wants to say, and why that reader will find it compelling. Map what the reader should think and feel. Cole calls this **Pinpoint Writing**, a specificity exercise with five questions:

- What problem am I solving?
- Whose problem am I solving?
- What emotion am I creating?
- What action am I encouraging?
- What benefit am I unlocking?

^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-turn-a-blank-page-into-a-finished-draft-in--2081358806408106390.md]

Boil the answers into the copywriting framework `How To {Outcome} Without {The Obstacle}`. Cole also phrases it as “How to {What does the reader want} without the (What’s the hard part for the reader}”. The source examples are:

- “How To Make Tons of Money Without Working 60 Hours Per Week”
- “How To Become A Digital Writer Without Spending $120K On An English Degree”
- “How To Write A First Draft Without The Pressure Of Perfection”

Cole says most first-draft time should be spent on this North Star. If the template cannot be filled in, the writer does not yet know what they are writing about and must resolve that first. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-turn-a-blank-page-into-a-finished-draft-in--2081358806408106390.md]

### 2. Bullet-point brain dump

Set a timer for **10 minutes** and write all the main ideas to cover, including random or seemingly unimportant concepts. Use bullet points and ignore grammar, spelling, and structure. Keep writing until the timer goes off; take a break and walk around for a few minutes if needed, then return to the list, group similar ideas, and turn the groups into an outline with main points and subpoints. The source calls outlining “90% of writing your first draft.” ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-turn-a-blank-page-into-a-finished-draft-in--2081358806408106390.md]

### 3. Develop more headline ideas

Review the brain dump and check every idea against the Step 1 North Star. Revise or remove ideas that no longer align. Then brainstorm **5 more** headline ideas, increasing the headline's “voltage” and specificity while keeping it short, concise, attention-grabbing, and accurate to the article's points. Iterate until one is preferred and choose the best headline. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-turn-a-blank-page-into-a-finished-draft-in--2081358806408106390.md]

### 4. Prep the page

Open a blank document, write the North Star or revised headline at the top, add the outline as subheads, and put the main points in a sensible order. The point is to make the next drafting step less intimidating before filling the page. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-turn-a-blank-page-into-a-finished-draft-in--2081358806408106390.md]

### 5. Fill in the skeleton

For each outline point, flesh out the details that help the reader understand it. Add steps, examples, reasons, mistakes, and stories to illustrate and clarify the ideas. The source links to an external Ship 30 for 30 article about adding depth and credibility to content; that destination was not fetched or evaluated. Work on one section at a time and do not wait for perfection—get the words on the page. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-turn-a-blank-page-into-a-finished-draft-in--2081358806408106390.md]

### 6. Marinate and publish

Step away from the draft and return after **a day or two** with fresh eyes. Cole's named example is his business partner `@dickiebush`: outline ideas on a walk every morning, fill the draft from **6 to 7:30 AM**, read it aloud and make necessary edits the next morning, then publish before feeling completely ready. The source's rationale is that every great piece of writing starts as a less-than-great draft. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-turn-a-blank-page-into-a-finished-draft-in--2081358806408106390.md]
