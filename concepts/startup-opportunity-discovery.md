# Startup Opportunity Discovery

A founder framework for finding opportunities before they look obvious. The core claim is that strong startups usually do not begin by inventing an entirely new market from nowhere; they begin with a non-consensus belief about a secular shift, a sharper wedge into an already-proven market, or a better fit for emerging power users.

## Core pattern

The article uses [[cursor]], [[figma]], and [[shopify]] as the main examples. In each case, the winning company was not first in the category. Instead it reinterpreted a market around a new behavior or infrastructure change:

- [[cursor]] bet that AI would reshape knowledge work and that developers would become the earliest high-intensity adopters.
- [[figma]] extended collaborative, cloud-native workflows from docs into design.
- [[shopify]] bet that merchants would increasingly want ownership of customer relationships instead of dependence on centralized marketplaces.

The practical founder lesson is to ask whether you are early in a shift, where usability for power users is the main opening, or later in a shift, where the opportunity is the "yin to the yang" that the first-generation winner missed.

## How opportunities are found

The source rejects armchair idea generation. Instead it recommends market immersion:

1. Use every meaningful product in the category.
2. Become a power user where possible.
3. Talk to loyal users, churned users, and dissatisfied edge-case users.
4. Look for workarounds that signal a genuine hair-on-fire problem.
5. Notice where incumbent success has slowed feedback loops or pushed products away from demanding users.

This overlaps with [[startup-launch-framework]], but the emphasis here is less on prepayment validation and more on developing differentiated market insight before the product feels inevitable.

## Product wedge and switching logic

A recurring test in the article is simple: why would someone switch? The recommendation is to avoid marginal improvements and instead deliver either a 10x gain or relief for an urgent problem. The article treats familiar workflow preservation as a major advantage: the best startups remove friction from behavior users already understand instead of forcing them to adopt an entirely new pattern.

That framing makes switching cost and distribution central, not secondary. A strong MVP is the smallest product wedge that gives users a compelling reason to leave what they already use, and a strong go-to-market motion earns attention where those users already spend time. This aligns naturally with [[distribution]].

## Distribution before inevitability

One of the article's sharper points is that distribution-market fit often comes before product-market fit. Founders must learn where customers already congregate, whose recommendations they trust, and what repetitive, unscalable actions are required to get the first users. In the examples cited, companies did not look inevitable while they were doing this work; they looked early, ignored, and sometimes wrong.
