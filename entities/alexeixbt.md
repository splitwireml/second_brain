## Source contribution

The article's core distinction is between a one-shot interaction, where immediate defection can look rational, and an iterated game with an unknown endgame, where today's exploited partner may be tomorrow's gatekeeper. It uses the Prisoner's Dilemma payoffs of one year for mutual silence, freedom for the betrayer plus ten years for the silent partner, and five years each for mutual betrayal to argue that predatory short-term wins can create a repeated punishment loop.

Alexei presents Robert Axelrod's 1980 repeated Prisoner's Dilemma tournament as the model's evidence: a reported four-line Tit for Tat program won in aggregate despite never crushing an individual opponent. The source describes four traits—100% initial cooperation without firing first, instant retaliation in the next round, total re-entry when cooperation resumes, and absolute clarity about triggers and rewards—as the basis for a predictable, hard-to-exploit environment.
