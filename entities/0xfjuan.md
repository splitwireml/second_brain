# Juan (@0xfJuan)

Juan is the author of a substantive local X Article describing a source-claimed four-stage system for making a technology-company launch trend on X: research, hook people, get attention, and conversion. The article says the author drove 6m+ impressions to tech companies in the prior two weeks and that one launch reached 2.4M impressions in its first 24 hours; these are source-reported claims, not independently verified results. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-get-your-launch-trending-on-x-full-guide-2082138861136736272.md]

## Source-described launch system

### 1. Research

The workflow begins with a Claude system built from X data. The source recommends twitterapi.io as a non-enterprise-API option, saying it provides a key in minutes, costs a fraction of a cent per tweet, and has no approval process. The study list contains 50 or more accounts serving the target audience: direct competitors, adjacent products, and every category company that launched within the last six months. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-get-your-launch-trending-on-x-full-guide-2082138861136736272.md]

The source then says to open Claude Code, pull recent posts from every study account, keep only posts that beat that account's own average views, and analyze the first line, opening claim, whether video or images carried the post, and the requested reader action. Claude writes one swipe file ranked by views with a note explaining why each post worked. The file is for patterns rather than sentences: reshape hooks that already worked on buyers, but write original copy. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-get-your-launch-trending-on-x-full-guide-2082138861136736272.md]

### 2. Hook people

The first ten seconds are treated as one unit: a 60–90 second video, the first line above it, and the reason to comment. The product's most impressive moment must appear within the first five seconds and be understandable with sound off; the source explicitly rejects leading with a logo or founder introduction. The first line should make a claim rather than describe, such as declaring the old way finished, claiming to be first at something new, or attacking a resented category practice. The claim should approach the edge of believable, then be tested on someone outside tech until they can repeat it over dinner. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-get-your-launch-trending-on-x-full-guide-2082138861136736272.md]

The first reply carries a lead magnet rather than the main post: a free playbook, template, breakdown, or early access in exchange for a comment. The source says this works close to 90% of the time and describes the comments as both lead collection and an expensive algorithmic signal arriving during the judgment window. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-get-your-launch-trending-on-x-full-guide-2082138861136736272.md]

### 3. Get attention

The pre-launch creator plan has three roles: big accounts for raw reach, mid-size niche experts for buyer trust, and hundreds of small accounts so the subject appears to have many voices. Each creator receives the exact posting minute, link, claim, and several angles; the angles prevent forty posts from looking like one press release, while each creator is told to write in their own voice rather than sound like a marketing department. Big accounts and experts post in the first hour; small accounts roll out in waves across the next three hours. The source's target is 200 or more posts across the first four hours, a pattern it says should look like platform discovery rather than a campaign. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-get-your-launch-trending-on-x-full-guide-2082138861136736272.md]

### 4. Conversion

When strangers post unprompted, DMs arrive, and the product is screenshotted into group chats, the founder replies to every comment with a real answer so each reply restarts the conversation. Everyone who shared or quoted the launch receives a same-day DM. Lead-magnet commenters receive the promised delivery quickly plus a question about what they are building. For a five-person team, the source's division is one person posting and four people working DMs. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-get-your-launch-trending-on-x-full-guide-2082138861136736272.md]

During the first 48 hours, the operator posts live milestones as plain numbers—signups, views, and waitlist size—replies publicly to late-arriving large accounts, and cuts new pieces from working material such as video clips, screenshots of unusual reactions, and a founder note. The source says to post nothing unrelated during that period, then remain visible for weeks because buyers may purchase later from the person they have continued seeing. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-get-your-launch-trending-on-x-full-guide-2082138861136736272.md]

## Launch recall and buyer-fit follow-through (2026-10-06)

Juan's later article extends the launch-day playbook with buyer memory and post-launch follow-through, detailed in [[viral-launch-system]]. He reports one post with **285K impressions and zero leads**, versus another with **55K impressions, four meetings, and one of those meetings turning into one of his biggest launches**. He also says he has spent **over $1M on X creators**. These are personal source-reported results and spending claims, not independently verified campaign data or general conversion benchmarks. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-make-your-launch-unforgettable-2107458102710735032.md]

The proposed workflow is to identify the buyer's future use-case trigger; make the product name and use case clear in the video without its caption; keep positioning consistent across founder post, demo, creator posts, and landing page while allowing each creator's own voice; and test company-name recall the next day on someone uninvolved in production. Creators receive early product access, demonstrate audience-specific workflows/problems, and are evaluated through target-market replies, use-case descriptions, access requests, and team discussion rather than views alone. Conversation-producing creators get a second post within **1–2 weeks**, with a different workflow, an audience-question answer, or a consenting user's example. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-make-your-launch-unforgettable-2107458102710735032.md]

Follow-up content draws on recurring questions, user creations, fixes, and requested demo detail; visible product updates become small launches. Returning buyers, recommendation tags, useful conversations, demo requests, and signups supply recall signals. Replies, DMs, sales calls, and **“Where did you hear about us?”** provide imperfect but useful attribution. The October article does not specify the earlier Claude/Claude Code/twitterapi.io research stack, an attribution implementation, or controlled evidence for these recommendations; its workflow remains a separate source-described marketing method. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-make-your-launch-unforgettable-2107458102710735032.md]

**Commercial framing:** Juan offers a personal **Loom video** launch breakdown by DM and asks readers to DM **RECALL** for his **Launch Recall Checklist**, covering checks before distribution spend and the **two weeks after launch**. For launches in the **next 30 days**, readers are asked what they are building and who it is for, with a promised first-focus recommendation. These are promotional CTAs, not delivered assets or independently established service capabilities; neither the checklist nor the Loom video is present in the local export. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-make-your-launch-unforgettable-2107458102710735032.md]

The July article's personal Loom offer and free-fit launch-service invitation are promotional framing, not verified service capabilities. ^[raw/articles/xarticle-how-to-get-your-launch-trending-on-x-full-guide-2082138861136736272.md]
