# How to Make Your Launch Unforgettable

How to Make Your Launch Unforgettable

I once had a post reach 285K impressions and bring in zero leads. Another reached 55K, booked four meetings, and one of those turned into one of our biggest launches.

If I had judged them by reach, I would have put more money behind the wrong post. The smaller one reached people who might actually buy what I sell. The same thing happens with startup launches. Thousands of people can watch your video, like the demo, and forget your company's name by the next day.

In one of my last articles, I wrote about how startups become "everywhere" on launch day. That kind of attention is useful. But a few weeks later, when someone asks their team for a product like yours, will anyone remember you?

Here's what I'd work on before the launch goes live.

# Know who needs to remember you

"Everyone in tech" is a tempting audience. It's also hard to make a useful impression on someone who has no reason to use your product.

Think about the situation that should bring your company to mind later.

- Maybe a developer is looking for a faster way to investigate failing tests.

- Maybe a support lead is tired of answering the same customer question for the fifth time that morning.

Now look at your launch from that person's perspective.

- Would they recognize their problem in the first few seconds?

- Would they know why your product is worth trying?

This should influence where you promote the launch, too. A creator with a smaller audience of people who face that problem may be more useful than a large account whose followers just enjoy watching AI demos.

# Make sure people remember the product

A good demo can travel far beyond your original post. People repost it, clip it, and drop it into group chats without the caption you spent hours writing.

Watch it that way. If someone sees only the video, can they tell which product it's from and what they can use it for?

- Put the product name where people will actually see it.

- Keep the description consistent across the founder's post, the demo, creator posts, and the landing page.

- Give creators room to speak in their own voice, but make sure they can explain what the product does accurately.

Before launch, show the asset to someone who hasn't worked on it.

Ask them about it the next day. If they remember the result but can't name the company, the video needs another pass.

# Choose creators you'd want to work with again

I've spent over $1M on X creators. One of the most expensive mistakes I see is treating each creator post as a one-time purchase.

Give creators access to the product early enough to find an angle that makes sense for their audience.

- Someone followed by developers might show a real workflow.

- Someone followed by founders might talk about the problem their team would use it to solve.

- A script that makes both accounts say the same thing wastes what you hired them for.

After launch, look beyond the view counts. Read the replies. Did people from your target market ask how it works? Did anyone describe a use case, request access, or bring it up with their team?

For the creators who started those conversations, plan a second post within the next week or two. Give them something new to show:

- A different workflow.

- An answer to a question their audience asked.

- An example from a user who has agreed to share it.

If that relationship works, keep going. People are more likely to associate a creator with your product when they see them return to it over time.

# Have something to say the following week

A lot of teams spend everything on the announcement.

By the following Monday, they're still reposting the same video because they haven't planned what comes next.

You don't need another expensive production. Look at what happened after people tried the product.

- Which question kept coming up?

- What did users make?

- What did your team fix?

- Which part of the demo did people want to see in more detail?

Those are possible posts for the next few weeks. A short screen recording that answers a real question can give someone a better reason to try the product than seeing the launch trailer again.

I like treating visible product updates as small launches. It gives the founder and team something concrete to talk about regularly, while giving interested buyers a reason to take another look.

# Check whether the right people are coming back

Launch-day impressions won't answer this on their own.

Pay attention to who mentions your product after the initial campaign. Are people tagging you when someone asks for a recommendation? Are potential buyers returning with specific questions? Do follow-up posts bring useful conversations, demo requests, or signups?

You can learn a lot from replies, DMs, sales calls, and a simple "Where did you hear about us?" question. You don't need perfect attribution to notice that one creator brought curious spectators while another brought people who wanted to use the product.

A launch can make people look at you for a day.

What you say, show, and ship afterward determines whether they think of you when they need what you sell.
