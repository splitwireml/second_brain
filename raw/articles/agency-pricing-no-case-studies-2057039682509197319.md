# How to Charge $2k+ for Your First Agency Client With Zero Case Studies

How to Charge $2k+ for Your First Agency Client With Zero Case Studies

The single biggest mistake new agency owners make is undercharging because they have no case studies yet.

The logic feels reasonable on the surface...

"I haven't proven myself, so I should charge less to make it easier for someone to take a chance on me." So they price their first offer at $300 a month, or $500 a month, or $1,000 for a one-time project, and they tell themselves they'll raise prices "once they have results."

This logic produces the exact opposite of the outcome they want.

Low prices attract the wrong clients. Low prices make your agency unprofitable from day 1. Low prices make it operationally impossible to deliver well, which produces poor results, which produces no case studies, which keeps you stuck at low prices indefinitely.

The agencies that escape this trap charge $2k+ for their first clients despite having zero case studies. Then they over-deliver, produce real results, build proper case studies, and use those results to grow into proper $5k+ DFY offers.

This article breaks down the complete pricing strategy for new agency owners who want to start at $2,000 to $3,000+ from day 1. The positioning shifts you need to make, the offer structures that justify the price even without proof, the sales call mechanics that close the deal, and the trade-offs you need to accept to make this work.

I built Paid House Media to over £2.5M in annual revenue and The Advertising Co to a multi-six-figure pacing within the first 3 months. Both businesses started with first clients paying $2,000 to $5,000 with no case studies in either niche. The framework below is what made that possible.

Let's get into it.

---

## Why $2k+ Specifically (And Not $500 or $1k)

Before getting into how to charge $2k+ without case studies, let's address why the price floor matters.

Pricing your first agency offer below $2,000 produces 4 specific problems that compound and kill the business.

Problem 1: The unit economics don't work. Even with zero ad spend and zero outbound infrastructure, you have real costs of delivery. Tools, time, sometimes contractor labour, software, the operational tax. A $500 a month retainer leaves no margin once you factor those in. You're essentially paying for the privilege of doing the work.

Problem 2: You attract the wrong clients. Low prices filter for prospects who can't afford much, which means they'll be price-sensitive on every interaction, push back on every additional cost, and produce significantly more operational friction than higher-paying clients. The prospects who pay $500 for a service are dramatically harder to serve than the prospects who pay $3,000 for the same service.

Problem 3: You can't reinvest in growth. Even when you start landing clients, $500 a month doesn't compound into the cash flow needed to invest in better tools, hire help, or eventually move to paid ads. You stay capped at owner-only fulfillment forever.

Problem 4: You can't deliver well. If your offer is $500 a month, you can spend maybe 2 to 4 hours per month on the client and still be profitable. That's not enough time to actually move the needle in most services. Underdelivery becomes inevitable, which produces churn, which prevents case study generation.

$2,000 is the realistic floor where unit economics start working, the client quality improves, and you have enough delivery budget to actually produce meaningful results.

This is true whether or not you have case studies. The economic math doesn't care about your credibility.

## The Reframe That Makes $2k+ Pricing Make Sense

Here's the mental shift most new agency owners need to make.

You're not charging $2,000 because you've proven you can deliver $20,000 in value (the standard pricing logic for established agencies). You're charging $2,000 because that's the minimum price at which you can actually deliver a quality service while building the business.

The price isn't about your worth. It's about the operational floor that makes the work viable.

Frame it this way on sales calls when prospects push back.

"My pricing reflects what it costs to deliver this service properly. At lower price points, agencies cut corners on strategy, on creative production, on reporting, on communication, and that's where the work breaks down. I'd rather charge what's required to do the work right than charge what's comfortable and then disappoint you."

This reframe shifts the conversation from "are you worth this" to "is the work worth doing properly." The first question is impossible to answer without case studies. The second question is much easier to answer because the prospect already wants the work done properly.

## The 3 Substitutes for Case Studies

You don't have case studies. That's fine. There are 3 substitutes that produce the same trust-building effect.

Substitute 1: Specific Methodology

A prospect evaluating an agency without case studies makes the decision based on the perceived quality of the methodology you'd apply to their problem.

If you can describe your approach in granular, specific, technical terms that demonstrate you genuinely understand the work, prospects often hire on methodology alone.

This means doing the work to actually develop a specific methodology before you start selling.

For an ads agency without case studies, methodology depth might look like:

"My process for a new account is to first run a 7-day diagnostic on your existing creative library to identify which angles are underperforming, then build a 14-day testing sprint with 8 to 12 new creatives across the 4 standard formats (founder, UGC, static, testimonial), then optimise based on the data using the Creative Testing tool inside Ads Manager. We focus exclusively on cost per booked call as the success metric, ignoring vanity metrics like CTR and CPM unless they're trending against the lead metric."

Compare that to:

"I run Facebook ads for B2B businesses."

The first version doesn't have case studies attached, but it sounds like someone who knows what they're doing. The second sounds like someone who watched a YouTube tutorial yesterday.

Methodology depth substitutes for case studies because prospects can verify your competence by listening to how specifically you talk about the work.

Substitute 2: Personal Authority

Even without case studies, you usually have something that signals authority in adjacent areas.

This might be:

- Previous experience working at an agency

- Time spent in-house running marketing for a brand

- A specific certification or training

- Years spent studying the discipline (even informally)

- A pattern of helping friends or smaller clients informally

- Personal results in your own related projects

Lead with whatever authority you do have. Even if it doesn't directly prove you can deliver agency results, it demonstrates you're not approaching the work cold.

The framing that works on sales calls:

"I haven't run a paid agency for years yet, but I've spent the last 18 months running ads for [your own e-commerce store / a friend's company / a side project], and the principles transfer directly. The methodology I'm proposing is the same one I've validated personally, just applied to your specific situation."

Prospects buy from people who feel like they know what they're doing. Authority can come from many sources, not just case studies.

Substitute 3: The Money-Back Guarantee

This is the most powerful substitute for case studies, and it's the one most new agency owners avoid because it scares them.

Offer a specific, time-bound, performance-tied money-back guarantee.

For an ads agency: "If we don't deliver [specific outcome] in 60 days, you get a full refund."

For an SEO agency: "If we don't move you into the top 10 for [specific keyword] in 90 days, you get a full refund."

For an email marketing agency: "If we don't 2x your email-attributed revenue in 60 days, you get a full refund."

The guarantee transfers all the risk from the prospect to you. Without case studies, the prospect's biggest fear is wasting money on someone who can't deliver. The guarantee removes that fear entirely.

Most new agency owners object to this with:

"What if I have to refund them? I can't afford to lose the money."

The reality: if you're picking the right clients, working in a niche you understand, and using a methodology that actually works, refund rates are typically below 10%. Even when refunds happen, you've spent 2 to 3 months actually doing the work and building skills, which is significantly more valuable than the $2,000 to $4,000 you might refund.

The guarantee isn't there because you expect to honour it. It's there because it makes prospects say yes when they otherwise would have walked.

## The Right ICP to Target Without Case Studies

Without case studies, you can't compete for the most attractive clients in any niche. Those clients have plenty of options and will gravitate toward agencies with deeper credibility.

You need to target a specific subset of clients where the case study gap matters less.

The right ICP for a new agency owner without case studies has 3 characteristics.

1. Smaller and earlier-stage businesses. A 7-figure brand with budget options will pick the agency with 50 case studies. A growing 6-figure brand without an established marketing function is more open to working with a newer agency, especially at the right price point.

2. Specific niches where competition is thinner. Avoid hyper-competitive niches like high-ticket coaching or e-commerce where dozens of established agencies are bidding for the same clients. Pick a niche where competitive density is lower. Plumbers, electricians, home service, accounting firms, regional B2B businesses, niche SaaS verticals.

3. Clients with adjacent existing relationships. Friends, friends-of-friends, businesses you've worked with before in any capacity, communities you're already part of. Warm-adjacent connections require less case-study-driven proof because there's pre-existing relationship trust.

Your first 3 to 5 clients should come from this filtered ICP.

Once you have those clients producing results, you have your case studies, and you can expand into more competitive ICP segments at higher price points.

The mistake new agency owners make is trying to target the same ICP as established agencies and competing on case studies they don't have. Pick a different battlefield instead.

## The Offer Structure That Justifies $2k+ Without Case Studies

Pricing is structural, not arbitrary. The offer needs to be constructed so that $2,000 to $3,000 feels appropriate to the prospect, even without case studies backing it up.

Here's the structure I recommend for new agency owners.

The 90-Day Sprint Structure

Don't offer ongoing retainers as your primary structure. Sell a 90-day sprint instead.

The framing: "I'm running a 90-day [outcome] sprint for [ICP] at $X. After the 90 days, if you want to continue, we move to an ongoing arrangement. If not, no pressure, no obligation."

The 90-day sprint structure produces 4 benefits when you have no case studies:

1. It feels less like a long-term commitment. Prospects without case study validation are scared of being locked into something indefinitely. A defined 90-day window feels safer.

2. It justifies a higher price point. A "90-day sprint" feels like a meaningful chunk of work. A "monthly retainer" feels like an ongoing tax. The same total dollars feel different in each framing.

3. It aligns with the realistic timeline of producing results. Most services need 60 to 90 days to show meaningful outcomes. The sprint window matches the delivery reality.

4. It creates a natural case-study generation moment. At day 90, you have a defined ending point where you can ask for a testimonial, document the results, and turn the engagement into a case study you'll use to land your next client.

The Deliverable Stack

Stack 8 to 12 specific deliverables into the offer so the perceived value massively exceeds the price.

For a $3,000 ads agency sprint, the deliverable stack might include:

- Complete ad account audit and strategy document

- Custom creative production (8 to 12 unique ads)

- Landing page audit and optimisation recommendations

- Conversion tracking setup (pixel, GTM, server-side if applicable)

- 90 days of campaign management and optimisation

- Weekly performance reports

- Weekly 30-minute strategy calls

- Custom audience research and competitor analysis

- Final case study document at the end of the engagement

The stacked deliverables make $3,000 feel like a steal. Prospects stop calculating "is this person worth $3k" and start feeling "I'm getting way more value than I'm paying for."

The Bonus Layer

Add bonuses that increase perceived value at zero marginal cost to deliver.

Possible bonuses for an agency owner without case studies:

- A 1-on-1 strategy call with you to map out their entire growth plan

- Access to your library of templates and SOPs

- Monthly office hours where you answer questions live

- A custom audit document at the end of the engagement

- Direct access to you via WhatsApp or iMessage during the engagement

Direct founder access is one of the strongest bonuses for new agency owners specifically because it's something established agencies usually can't offer. Use the smallness of your operation as a feature, not a bug.

## The Sales Call Mechanics for First Clients

The structure of the sales call matters more without case studies because you can't rely on proof to do the closing work.

The call framework I recommend.

Section 1: Discovery (20 to 30 minutes)

Spend significantly longer than usual on discovery. Ask granular questions about their current situation, their previous attempts, their goals, their fears, their constraints.

The deeper you understand their specific problem, the less your lack of case studies matters. They're not buying based on "have you done this 100 times before." They're buying based on "do you understand my specific situation."

Discovery without case studies needs to be 2x as deep as discovery with case studies.

Section 2: Methodology Walkthrough (15 to 20 minutes)

Walk them through your specific methodology applied to their situation. Don't speak in generalities. Reference their specific industry, their specific competitors, their specific data points.

"Based on what you've shared about your current setup, the specific approach I'd take is..."

Then go granular. The granularity is what substitutes for case studies in the prospect's perception.

Section 3: Offer Reveal (5 to 10 minutes)

Present the offer with the full deliverable stack, the price, the guarantee, the timeline.

Frame the guarantee prominently. The guarantee is doing most of the closing work in the absence of case studies.

"Here's what's included in the 90-day sprint... Here's the price... And here's my guarantee. If we don't deliver [specific outcome] in [timeframe], you get a full refund. So your risk in this engagement is zero. The only risk is whether we don't deliver, and if we don't, you're whole."

Section 4: Objection Handling (10 to 15 minutes)

The main objections from prospects without case studies in front of them:

"I want to think about it." Reframe as a specific concern. "What specifically would you want to think through? I'd rather address it now than have you go away with an unanswered question."

"Do you have other clients you've done this for?" Be honest. "I haven't run a full agency engagement for an [niche] before, but my methodology is grounded in [adjacent experience]. That's exactly why I'm offering the guarantee, so you're not taking the risk of someone newer."

"It feels expensive." Reframe to delivery reality. "$3,000 over 90 days is $1,000 a month, or $33 a day. The alternative is doing this yourself, which costs you significantly more in time, or hiring someone cheaper, which costs you in delivery quality. I priced this at the floor where I can actually deliver the work properly."

Section 5: Close (5 to 10 minutes)

Direct close. Don't dance around it.

"Based on everything we've discussed, this seems like a good fit. Are you ready to move forward?"

If yes, process the payment on the call. Never end a closed sales call without taking the deposit. Prospects who walk away "to think about it after agreeing" frequently never come back.

If no, identify the specific objection and address it.

## The Trade-Off You Need to Accept

Charging $2k+ without case studies works, but it requires accepting a specific trade-off.

You're going to have lower close rates than agencies with case studies.

A new agency owner running the framework above might close 10 to 15% of sales calls in the first 90 days. An established agency with strong case studies will close 25 to 35% of comparable calls.

This is fine. You just need to book more calls to compensate.

Specifically: where an established agency might book 20 calls to sign 5 clients, you'll need to book 40 to 50 calls to sign 5 clients in the same window.

The implication: your front-end activity (content, outbound, networking, whatever you're using to generate calls) needs to be roughly 2x more intense in the first 90 days than the long-term steady state.

Most new agency owners don't accept this trade-off. They try to maintain comfortable activity levels and then complain that close rates are low. The fix isn't lowering the price. The fix is increasing the activity to compensate for the lower close rate while you build the case studies that will eventually raise your close rate.

After 90 days of running this approach, you should have 3 to 5 paying clients, 1 to 2 of whom are producing real results worth documenting. Those become your first case studies. Your close rate climbs. Your activity requirement decreases. The system compounds.

## The 90-Day Trajectory With This Approach

If you follow this playbook for 90 days, here's the realistic outcome.

Days 1 to 30: Tighten the offer, build the methodology document, set up the sales call framework, start generating call volume through whatever channel you've committed to (content, outbound, network outreach). Expect 0 to 2 clients signed.

Days 30 to 60: Sales calls compound. You've refined your pitch from real call data. Start closing 1 in 10 calls. Expect 2 to 4 clients signed by day 60.

Days 60 to 90: Initial clients producing results. Testimonials and partial case studies starting to come through. Close rate begins climbing to 1 in 7 or 1 in 6. Expect 4 to 7 clients signed by day 90.

Days 90 to 180: Real case studies from the first 3 to 5 engagements. Close rate climbs further. Pricing can be raised on new clients ($3k becomes $4k, then $5k). Foundation laid to expand into paid ads acquisition once revenue supports it.

The 90-day mark is the critical inflection point. That's when you have enough proof to graduate from "agency owner with no case studies" to "agency owner with early case studies," which dramatically improves the rest of the journey.

## What Not to Do

A few specific things to avoid:

Don't price test downward. When a prospect pushes back on $3k, don't drop to $2k to save the deal. Either hold the price or walk. Dropping the price once signals you'll drop it again, and the rest of the relationship is poisoned.

Don't offer free work to build a case study. This sounds reasonable but produces 2 problems. First, free work attracts the worst clients (the ones who weren't going to pay anyway). Second, free clients don't take the work seriously and rarely produce the kind of engaged outcomes that make for strong case studies.

Don't apologise for your lack of case studies. Most new agency owners spend significant call time defending their inexperience. Stop doing this. Confidence in your methodology substitutes for confidence in your track record. Prospects sense which one you have.

Don't undercharge to "build credibility." Credibility isn't built by being cheap. It's built by doing the work properly with clients who pay enough that you can actually deliver.

Don't accept clients you know you can't help. The fastest way to destroy your reputation as a new agency owner is to take clients in niches you don't understand or with goals you can't realistically deliver. The wrong client kills you twice: once when they don't get results, and once when they tell everyone in their network.
