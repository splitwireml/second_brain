# The Complete Cold Email Playbook for 2026 (It's a Distribution Game Now)

The Complete Cold Email Playbook for 2026 (It's a Distribution Game Now)

I sat down with Charles Tenot, the CEO who grew @lemlist from $15M to $53M ARR, and Erwan, his VP of Growth. We compared notes on what is booking meetings right now: their view from a platform that sees 20,000 signups a month, and mine from the hundreds of B2B clients we ran outbound for when ColdIQ was still an agency (if you didn’t know, we have a unified GTM API now, try it at [coldiq.com](http://coldiq.com/)).

What follows is that conversation rebuilt as a playbook, seven steps in the order I would run them. If you’re looking for a distribution playbook based off of the experience of some of the biggest players, you are in the right place. Read on.

---

# Step 1: Run every campaign on two rails

Across what is working right now, on their side and ours, the same two habits keep showing up.

- Rail one is hyper-personalization. The real kind starts long before the writing: proper data enrichment on every row, lists built only from people who genuinely fit your ICP, and one campaign per segment instead of one campaign for everyone. A first name dropped into a merge tag does not count anymore. Buyers have seen ten thousand of those, and the delete key is muscle memory by now.

- Rail two is multi-channel. Flooding inboxes is cheap, which is exactly why an inbox alone cannot win.

> If I am in your mailbox with ten of your competitors, why would you respond to me and not them?

The honest answer is recognition.

Email gives you the reach. LinkedIn is where the trust gets built, because a prospect who checks the sender and finds a real person with a real audience treats the message differently. The brand on top is what makes your name register at all when it sits beside ten strangers asking for the same meeting.

We measured what this does to results across our own client base, and the number was blunt: clients who run multi-channel outreach stay with us 2.5x longer than clients on email alone. They stay because the meetings show up.

Do this: attach a LinkedIn touch from a real profile to every email sequence you run. Prospects click the sender's name before they decide anything. What they find on the other end of that click is part of your campaign, whether you planned it or not.

---

# Step 2: Treat LinkedIn as an asset, and mine the engagement

A LinkedIn account with a real audience behaves like an asset, and it cannot be replicated overnight. Fake accounts get caught fast. A profile that only sends DMs, never posts, and never comments is an easy tell, so the ban arrives early and takes the pipeline down with it. Even the sophisticated fake-account vendors only buy you time. Spend the same effort enabling a real person on your team instead.

The part almost nobody uses is what a real audience produces every single day: engagement data. It is the warmest intent signal you can access, and it sits in your notifications for free.

Erwan tested how warm it really is.

His team exported everyone in his first-degree network who had engaged with his posts, and a rep called each one, opening with the post they had just reacted to.

> Roughly 95% of them answered and talked.

That is an answer rate on the phone, not a reply rate in an inbox.

He was upfront that it does not scale, and also that it did not need to. Even when the person was the wrong fit, the calls produced referrals and honest market intel no cold list would ever surface.

There is a second layer worth building once the export habit exists. Track which topics each person engages with. Someone who reacts every time you post about AI in outbound is telling you which conversation to open with them. A person who only ever opens your deliverability content needs a different first line entirely. Their engagement history writes your personalization for you.

Do this, weekly: export the people who engaged with your posts, match them against your ICP, and reach out with the post they touched as your opener. Start with first-degree connections.

---

# Step 3: Split the work between AI and humans

The teams getting real results with AI in outbound have all landed on the same division of labor.

- AI takes the heavy lifting. Scraping, enrichment, list building, scoring against your ICP, intent detection, campaign analytics. Work that used to need a dedicated team crunching rows now runs unattended, and it is the one place where AI is clearly better than people.

- Humans keep the words. Charles has watched thousands of campaigns move through Lemlist, and he has still never seen AI write a cold email as well as a strong human writer. AI copy leaves a fingerprint, and inboxes have learned to read it.

His explanation for why stayed with me:

> AI is statistics. Being creative is doing the opposite of statistics.

I keep meeting the proof from the other direction. People show me content systems that write posts on autopilot, and the dashboards always look impressive. Then I check the actual posts and they sit at 15 likes. On any given morning I can write something off the top of my head and outperform the entire system, because a system built on statistics produces the statistical average by design.

Do this: automate everything that happens before the message exists. Then hand the message itself to a human, and give that human twice the writing time they used to get. Spend the hours AI saves you on the writing itself.

---

# Step 4: Build signals nobody else has

Intent signals have a maturity problem that almost nobody inside the industry says out loud. Everyone subscribes to the same handful: fundraising announcements, new-role alerts, hiring sprees. The week a company raises, a hundred congratulation emails land in the founder's inbox at once. The signal was real, and it is worthless anyway, because you are the ninety-ninth person acting on it.

The signals that still convert are the ones you invent. A few real ones that came up when we compared notes:

- Their email landed in your spam folder. A company sitting in your spam is already paying for outbound, and the outbound is broken. Lemlist merges the spam folders of the whole team into one list and reaches out to every company that appears in it.

- A decision maker at a target account starts connecting with your competitor's reps. A buying project is live, the evaluation is happening this month, and almost nobody watches for it.

- Their ad spend just jumped. One paid-ads agency tracks rising Meta budgets and reaches out the same week the budget grows, at the exact moment performance is on the prospect's mind.

- Their web traffic is spiking. For a bot-detection product this is the entire pitch, because some of that new traffic is probably bots, and the email arrives while the spike is still on their dashboard.

- DMARC is missing on their domain. A company without basic email authentication has nobody thinking about deliverability, and that gap is exactly what certain vendors fix.

None of these came from a data vendor's catalog.

Each one was reverse-engineered from a real reason a customer bought. That is the actual method, and it is repeatable any week you choose:

1. Call your last 20 customers. Ask what was happening in their business when they signed, and why they moved when they did.

2. Read your sales call transcripts and hunt for the sentence that keeps repeating across deals.

3. When seven prospects out of ten name the same trigger, that trigger is your signal. Find the public data point that exposes it, and aim your targeting there.

This is how I found ours.

Posting about AI earned me the engagement, but the people actually booking calls kept mentioning the same problem: their email was landing in spam. Engagement pointed one way and revenue pointed somewhere else. The spam pattern became a targeting rule we still run today.

Everyone can buy the same data, and nobody can buy your closed-won evidence.

# Step 5: Write before you scale

The tooling makes it possible to automate a campaign on day one, and that convenience quietly ruins more campaigns than bad copy does. The order matters more than the tooling. Scale only what the writing has already proven.

Write 35 to 50 messages by hand before anything gets automated.

Hand-writing is where the patterns surface. You start noticing which data points mark a lead who replies, which angle earns a response, and which lines felt dead the moment you typed them. Once a pattern proves itself across 50 manual sends, scaling it to a thousand is the easy part. Automating earlier than that just multiplies a guess.

One line from inside Lemlist has stuck with me since:

> Send your outreach email to yourself first.

Read your own campaign the way a stranger would. Most senders would never reply to their own email, and the test costs nothing.

There is a curve underneath all of this that explains where the effort belongs.

Reply rates climb slowly as an email improves from a 5 to an 8, and then somewhere past 9 they multiply. The estimate that came out of our discussion: moving a campaign from an 8 to a 9 can potentially change replies a hundredfold, because 9 is the point where a message stops blending in. An 8 earns nearly the same silence as a 6, so the hours sunk into polishing a 7 still buy an email that disappears.

Tom Greenwood, a rep Charles rates among the best in the game, lives at the far end of that curve. He records a personal video for each tier-one prospect, walking through their website on camera and pointing at the places they lose money. Every video costs him 15 to 20 minutes, and his campaigns pull around a 30% reply rate from exactly the accounts everyone else fails to reach. The meetings convert too, because the person booking had a real reason to show up.

Do this: pour the slow, deep effort into your tier-one accounts and let the results teach you what works. Carry the lesson down-market once you have it.

---

# Step 6: Earn the open with a pattern interrupt

Charles thinks about outbound the way you would think about street advertising. You pass a hundred posters without registering a single one, and then one stops you mid-stride. Most cold emails going unnoticed is normal, and it is survivable. The job is to be the one that gets noticed this week.

Two subject lines that pulled it off:

- A phone-data provider put the prospect's own number in the subject with the words "Is this you?" The product proved itself before the email was even opened.

- "Is this your email?" with a screenshot of the prospect's own message sitting in a spam folder.

Both land for one reason: they demonstrate instead of claim.

Every interrupt expires.

Short lowercase subjects worked until every inbox filled with them. Voice notes were fresh for a year, then turned into the pattern themselves.

Personalized images made Lemlist famous once, everyone copied them, and like fashion they may even come back around. Nothing on this list is permanent, and that is the point of the exercise.

Do this: open your own inbox and study the first twenty cold emails inside it. Write down what they have in common. Build your next subject line to break the exact pattern you just documented.

# Step 7: Close the loop so the engine improves itself

Everything above gets an engine running. This step is what makes it improve while it runs, and it is where both of our teams are heading.

The wiring already exists through MCPs in Claude Code.

Your meeting recorder holds what buyers actually said on every call. Your sequencer knows who replied, on which channel, at which step. Your CRM knows what closed and why. Connect them and a loop forms:

1. Calls get recorded, and the transcripts pile up as raw evidence of what buyers want.

2. Patterns get found. Which persona replies at triple the rate of the rest, which objection keeps coming up, and which buying reason repeats across closed deals.

3. Campaigns get updated, so the next send targets the persona that replies, written in the words buyers used on the calls.

4. Results feed back in, and every campaign starts smarter than the one before it.

I used to run the second stage by hand.

I would send a campaign to a broad sales persona, watch the replies come in, and notice something like sales ops answering at three times the rate of everyone else.

The next campaign would target sales ops only. That entire analysis now happens inside the loop on its own, and it reaches further than I ever did, because it reads the call transcripts at the same time and connects what people replied to with what they said once they showed up.

One play from this loop you can copy today is the lost-deal reactivation. Erwan pulled the transcripts of deals Lemlist lost six months ago, found the specific objection inside each one, and re-reached out: at the time you told us the product was missing something, and that something has now shipped. He ran it last week, and the replies were already arriving.

From our side at ColdIQ, the ceiling looks like this: our GTM engineers prompt an entire campaign build, leave for breakfast, and come back thirty minutes later to review a finished draft with the list uploaded and every touchpoint placed. Around 1,500 companies already run Lemlist through Claude each month.

The human's job moved from doing the work to judging it, and quality went up, because judging is the part humans were always better at.

# Closing Note

I think any good guide should also come with a list of things to avoid, and here’s mine:

Fake LinkedIn accounts. They get banned quickly, they carry zero proof when a prospect clicks through, and keeping one alive takes the same effort as enabling a real teammate, for a fraction of the return.

Building your own sending infrastructure because AI can code now. The chain is predictable. The vibe-coded sender works, then lands in spam, then needs secondary mailboxes, then warmup logic, then rotation, and a few weeks later you have stitched together a Frankenstein that a $100-a-month tool already outperforms. Lemlist's warmup layer alone watches 50,000 domains and corrects deliverability shifts within 24 hours. Custom software belongs in your internal workflows. The infrastructure your revenue rides on is worth renting from a company whose entire business is maintaining it.

Scaling something that never worked small. The tools remove every barrier to automating on day one, and that ease is the trap itself. Scale multiplies whatever already exists. When what exists is a guess, volume just delivers the guess to more people.
