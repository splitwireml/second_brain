# /success-metric: never ever worry again about setting the success metric as a PM

/success-metric: never ever worry again about setting the success metric as a PM

A launch review is the wrong place to decide what success means.

By then, people can defend several charts. Activation may have risen while support tickets rose too and retention stayed flat. One person points to the number from the kickoff, while another notes that nobody agreed it would decide the result.

The charts may all be accurate. They still do not settle the decision.

The decision happened before the work began, when the team either wrote down what would count as success or left room to negotiate it later.

## Put the decision on one page

A one-page success-metric document needs enough detail that two people cannot read it differently. It names the user outcome the team is trying to cause and the one metric that decides whether the change worked. It also records the baseline, minimum acceptable result, target, review date, and harms that would make an apparent win unacceptable.

Start with the outcome, not the metric. "Improve onboarding" is an intention. "A new workspace admin creates a project and invites a teammate without help" is an event someone could watch happen.

Then define the deciding metric. “Activation rate” is only a label until a team names the numerator, denominator, population, and time window. If those fields are loose, the metric will change meaning when the review becomes contentious.

Supporting metrics still matter because they explain the result. They should not overrule the metric the team chose in advance.

## The number is not enough

A target without a baseline is a guess with a percentage sign attached. When a team does not know the current value, measuring it may be the first piece of work.

The minimum acceptable result needs its own field. A target says what the team wants. The minimum says what result makes the change worth keeping. Writing both before launch prevents the review from becoming a hunt for a lower standard that makes the project look successful.

The review date matters for the same reason. A result is not ready because a stakeholder asks for it on Tuesday. It is ready when enough of the right users have had enough time to produce a meaningful signal.

## Look for the way it could go wrong

I think this question does most of the work: how could the number improve while users get worse off?

A team might make onboarding look better by automatically creating a sample project. The activation metric rises. But if those projects sit untouched, the product has not helped anyone get started. It has made the dashboard easier to celebrate.

That is what guardrails are for. Name the harm, decide how the team will detect it, and set the threshold before the launch gives anyone an interest in ignoring it. A few well-chosen guardrails are enough. A longer list of measures often makes it easier to avoid a decision.

## Use AI to challenge the contract

AI cannot choose the outcome, the acceptable trade-off, or the moment a team should admit a change failed. Those are product judgments.

It can make the preparation less tedious. Ask it to challenge the metric, list ways the team could accidentally game it, check whether the definition has all four fields, and estimate whether the data will support a review on the planned date.

That turns the work from an empty document into a structured argument the PM can inspect and own.
