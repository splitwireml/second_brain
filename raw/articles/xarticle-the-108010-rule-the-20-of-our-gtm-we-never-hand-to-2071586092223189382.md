# The 10·80·10 Rule: The 20% of Our GTM We Never Hand to AI

The 10·80·10 Rule: The 20% of Our GTM We Never Hand to AI

I've watched a lot of operators wire AI into their outbound this year and end up worse off than before they started. They have the same tools and models, they're shipping more, and the pipeline is flatter than before.

The pattern is almost always the same.

They let AI take over the calls that needed their judgment, and spent their own days on the busywork it should have absorbed.

We've run cold email, LinkedIn, and cold calling for 400+ B2B companies on the way to $7M ARR at ColdIQ. More than 23 million cold emails across those campaigns. About 30 people producing what would normally take a company several times the size. What made that work was being deliberate about the one slice we never automate.

That slice is the 20% that takes judgment. AI runs the other 80%, the part that just takes hours. I lay it out as 10·80·10: the first 10% is yours, the middle 80% is the machine's, the last 10% is yours again.

---

# The 80% got cheap this year

This matters more now than it did even a year ago, because that middle 80% just went cheap for everyone. The research, the enrichment, the first drafts, the follow-ups: the layer that used to need a pod of SDRs and a week of their time now runs overnight for the cost of the API calls.

Your competitor has the same models you do. They can research the same 500 accounts and write the same 500 opening lines by morning.

When everyone can do that work, it stops being an advantage.

> So the value moved to the two things AI still can't do: decide who's worth chasing in the first place, and judge whether what came back is good enough to send. That's the 20%.

---

# The rule, in one line

Hand the middle to AI and keep those two decisions for yourself: the targeting call at the front, and the quality check at the end.

They are a small share of the hours, and they decide whether the other 80% was worth running at all.

---

# Leg 1: You decide (the first 10%)

Before a tool runs, someone has to make the calls that set the ceiling on everything downstream. A model can speed this up. It can't make the call for you.

Three things sit here:

- Who you go after, and why now

- What a strong-fit account looks like

- The angle and offer you lead with

Say you point it at Series-B SaaS companies that just hired a VP of Sales.

That one decision moves your reply rate more than any subject line the machine writes three steps later, and a prompt can't hand it to you. On most accounts we cut up to 40% of the addressable market on purpose, because the fastest way to book more meetings is to email fewer people who are ready to buy.

When we replaced cold dialing at ColdIQ with a tiered signal score, it pulled five times the meetings off a smaller list. A cold list replies a few percent of the time. The same outreach fired on a real buying signal replies at 15 to 25%. You win that gap up front, before the machine sends a thing.

The list is the strategy.

---

# Leg 2: AI does the work (the 80%)

The middle is where AI earns its keep.

It pulls the data on every company, writes the first draft of each message, and keeps the sends and follow-ups running. This is the part that used to eat your week. Now it runs overnight.

At ColdIQ the repetitive layer is staffed by software top to bottom, which is the only reason about 30 people can push more than 500,000 cold emails a month at peak. Clay handles most of the structured work, and anything off the beaten path gets scripted once and reused.

One warning, because it's the most expensive mistake I see.

Handing the middle to AI is right. Skip the foundation under it and you wake up to a wrecked domain. A "fully autonomous" agent that hits a deliverability dip at 3am, panics, and torches a client's sending reputation by morning had no brakes on it, because nobody set the fences first.

Setting those fences is your job, even when the middle runs itself.

---

# Leg 3: You check it (the last 10%)

Then it comes back to you, and you read it before any prospect does.

Most of the output looks finished even when it isn't, so you read for the lines the model got wrong or invented, anything off-tone for your brand, and which messages are good enough to send. Out of those 500 overnight drafts, you cut the 12 that missed and send the 488 that landed. The edit takes a few minutes and saves you the account that the bad 12 would have cost.

We bake this into our own content operation.

A grader scores every draft against a 50-point rubric, and anything under 38 goes back for a rewrite before a person ever touches it. AI drafts, the human sharpens, the voice profile is the guardrail. That setup pushed out 581 posts in 87 days and drove $153K in new MRR, and a person still reads the last stretch before anything goes live.

It is the cheapest check in the whole system. People still drop it the moment they get busy.

---

# How to set the split up in your own stack

You don't need new software to run this. You need three things, and you can build them this week.

A decision doc for the front 10%. Who you're targeting and why now, what a good-fit account looks like, the one angle you're leading with. That's the brief the machine runs against. If it only lives in your head, neither the machine nor the next person on the account can inherit your judgment.

A foundation for the middle 80%. Rules the agent can't override, hard sending limits, and a kill switch for the moment deliverability slips. Let it run on its own, inside limits you set up front. The 3am domain story is what happens without them.

A review gate for the back 10%. Decide before launch what gets checked and what bar it has to clear: a score, a tone pass, a fact pass on anything the model personalized. Keep the gate cheap enough that you run it on every batch instead of skipping it when you're busy.

Set those three up once and most of this runs itself, with your attention on the two ends and the machine on the middle.

---

# Why the 20% is what counts now

Most people run this backwards.

They pour their judgment into tuning automations that already run fine, then rubber-stamp the targeting and the output, where the judgment was the whole job.

The 80% is table stakes now that everyone's tools can do it. What you still own is the call on who's worth chasing and the read on what's good enough to send under your name, and neither one scales with compute.

So before you wire one more agent into your stack, look at where your attention is actually going. Give the machine the work and keep the judgment. The 20% is still yours to get right.
