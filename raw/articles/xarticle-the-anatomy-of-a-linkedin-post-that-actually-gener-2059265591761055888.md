# The Anatomy of a LinkedIn Post That Actually Generates Leads

The Anatomy of a LinkedIn Post That Actually Generates Leads

Most people who post on LinkedIn never generate a single lead from it. They show up for six months, write technically correct posts, get a handful of likes from coworkers, and quietly decide LinkedIn doesn't work for their business. Then they pivot back to running ads.

The post itself is almost always what's broken. Not the topic or the timing or the algorithm. The actual unit of content you publish.

When LinkedIn surfaces a post to someone scrolling at 8:42am between meetings, that person spends less than a second deciding whether to slow down. If the post doesn't earn that second, nothing downstream matters. Audience size becomes irrelevant at that point, and so do hashtags, CTAs, and the time of day you hit publish. You lost them before they read a single word.

This is an anatomy of how a high-performing LinkedIn post is engineered.

The visual decisions, the copy patterns, the structural rules, the metric you optimize for. I sat down with [Pierre Herubel](https://www.linkedin.com/in/pierre-herubel-540b3949/)(170K follwers), who runs Content Path and is one of the most-followed B2B marketing creators in the space, and pulled apart his workflow.

We've installed a version of this across 24+ ColdIQ team members posting from their own profiles. LinkedIn drives roughly 65% of our inbound and $80K MRR on its own.

---

# 95% of the performance lives inside the image

When you attach a visual to a LinkedIn post, the visual is the post. The hook complements it, the body complements it, but both amplify what the image already did or didn't do.

This isn't a vibe. It's the pattern that emerges when you study posts that consistently land above 1,500 engagements versus posts that flatline. The image is what makes someone stop scrolling. It's what gets screenshotted into a Notes app for a meeting next Thursday, and what gets reposted with a one-line caption from someone else's profile. Everything else compounds on top of whether the visual earned a pause.

What this means is that you reverse your order of operations. You don't draft a post and then ask whether to add a visual. You design the visual first, and the copy is what you write around it.

---

## The four infographic patterns that compound

If you study the highest-performing posts on B2B LinkedIn, almost all of them collapse into four visual archetypes. We use variants of all four internally.

1. The comparative table is two columns side by side. The wrong way on the left, the right way on the right. B2B vs B2C marketing, demand gen vs lead gen, old outbound stack vs new outbound stack. Comparative tables work because the human brain processes contrast faster than any other relationship. A reader doesn't need to read both sides to feel the asymmetry.

2. The pyramid or funnel stacks three to five horizontal layers vertically, each layer being a stage of something. Four levels of B2B marketing. Five stages of pipeline. The pyramid format tells the brain there's an order to the chaos, and that order is something the reader wants to learn.

3. The iceberg shows the surface, then everything below the surface. "What founders think outbound is" above the waterline. "What outbound actually is" below it, taking up five times the space. Icebergs are the single most copied visual format on LinkedIn for a reason. The metaphor is doing all the work.

4. The visual metaphor pairs two opposing images. A Rubik's cube next to a single button. A maze next to a straight line. Pierre's most copied post pairs a glowing button labeled "what people think marketing is" against a Rubik's cube labeled "what marketing actually is." The metaphor finishes the argument before the post begins.

Pick one of these four and vary the topic across it.

The compounding comes from getting very good at a small number of patterns, not from inventing a new visual format every week.

# How to actually build the image

Open Canva on a 1200×1200 square canvas. LinkedIn crops anything taller into the feed view, so square gives you the most usable real estate. Budget twenty to sixty minutes per infographic, and if you find yourself past sixty, you're overdesigning.

The build sequence is the same every time.

A title bar runs across the top in a bold sans-serif at roughly 45pt, and the title itself is one short clause rather than a sentence. "Outbound: what most founders get wrong" works. "Here's what most founders get wrong about outbound in 2026" does not.

Below the title sits the core element, which is your chosen archetype.

For comparisons, press L for a vertical divider line and T for text blocks on each side. For pyramids, use stacked rectangles from the Elements panel and delete the default labels so you can write your own. Keep each entry to keywords only, where "big untargeted list" beats "a massive list of leads that don't match your ICP," and cap entries at five per side, with three being the sweet spot. If you have nine bullets, you've combined two frameworks and need to split them into two posts.

Color codes the conclusion. 

One color for the wrong column, usually red or orange, one for the right column, usually green or blue. Readers feel which side wins without reading. Two small contextual labels finish the visual, like an arrow saying "Most teams start here" pointing at the wrong column, and a "What actually works" label above the right side. Those two labels do 80% of the explaining for you.

The whole build takes about twenty minutes once you have template muscle memory. The first three you make will take an hour, and the tenth will take twenty.

# How readers actually consume the post

Once the image stops the scroll, the reader follows a predictable path.

They read the title of the infographic first, because it's the biggest text on screen. Their eye drops top to bottom. They slow down on internal details if the framework feels valuable. Only then do they click "see more" to read the post body. By the time someone is reading word 14 of your hook, you've already won the impressions battle. The body is for converting attention into action, not for capturing it.

> Your job isn't to make people read every word. Your job is to engineer where they drop off, and to make every drop-off point still worth their time.

A reader who drops off after the image got the framework, which is a save. A reader who drops off after the hook got the thesis, which is a like. A reader who reads to the end got the playbook, which is a comment or a DM.

Each level still produces a result if you've designed for it.

# The hooks that earn the "see more" click

LinkedIn surfaces the first two lines of your post in the feed. That's the entire window you have to earn the expansion. Pierre and our team both use two hook archetypes.

- The negative hook is problem-first, and we use it 60 to 70% of the time. You name a problem your reader is actively living with, then promise the real principle in the second line. A version we run constantly looks like this: "Too many founders make this mistake: they rush to tactics before fixing their positioning. The real problem most are missing is..." The reader's curiosity is now load-bearing, and they have to expand the post to resolve it.

- The positive hook opens with a specific credential and earns the click only when that credential is specific enough to feel unfair. "I've been in marketing for ten years" is worse than no credential at all. "I've analyzed 1,000,000 cold emails" earns the click. The more granular the number, the heavier the authority.

Either way, the hook has exactly one job: open a curiosity gap, then deliver the answer in the very next sentence after "see more." Pierre calls a hook that promises one thing and resolves the answer fifteen lines down a deceiving hook, and it kills more posts than weak openings ever do.

The PAS framework (problem, agitate, solution) holds most of this together.

Name the problem in the hook, make it sting in the next paragraph, deliver the solution in the second half. We use PAS on roughly 60% of our posts because it maps cleanly to how a buyer's brain moves through awareness, urgency, and intent.

# The body: rhythm beats density

The most underrated variable in a LinkedIn post is what the body looks like on screen. Three dense paragraphs of running text feel like a tax to read, and the reader bounces before they've parsed sentence one. The same content broken into rhythmic chunks reads as effortless.

Sentence rhythm matters more than sentence quality.

Mix a six-word punch with a fifteen-word explanation, then a single-word emphasis, then a twenty-word analytical line. Uniform rhythm in either direction reads as effort. Use single-line paragraphs, two-line breaks between thoughts, and a bolded keyword every three or four sentences. A reader skimming should still extract 70% of the value from what stands out visually.

Most of our posts fit inside 1,300 characters and around 140 words. That's the structural budget. LinkedIn truncates aggressively past that, and readers bounce. The shape we copy across most posts is simple.

> One line restates the hook payoff and delivers the principle. Two or three lines expand it with a single example. Three or four lines cover the actionable breakdown. One final line ties back to the principle.

## The closing: never end with "DM me"

This is the single biggest pattern we've changed at ColdIQ. The default LinkedIn ending is some version of "PS: DM me if you want help with this." We stopped doing it almost entirely on educational posts. Pierre never does it.

The closing line sets the tone of the entire post.

When a post teaches something useful for nine paragraphs and the tenth asks for a meeting, the whole post collapses backward in the reader's memory and rewrites itself as marketing. Trust evaporates.

Two patterns consistently work in its place.

- The first is a principle restatement, like "once you figure out the demand generation part, the rest is just focus, investment, and time."

- The second is an opinion or point of view that invites a comment without explicitly asking for one, like "most teams will keep optimizing send time when the real lever is the list, and that's the gap most agencies sell into." Both keep the post inside the education frame, which is the frame that compounds reach.

The sales conversations come from cumulative weight, not from CTAs on individual posts. We've tracked this. Our LinkedIn channel produced 1,000+ meetings and $2.2M+ ARR in 2024, and almost none of those meetings were booked from the bottom of a post. They were booked because someone had seen 12 educational posts before they reached out cold.

# Optimize for saves, not just likes

The most underweighted metric on LinkedIn is the save.

Saves correlate with dwell time, which is the strongest input the algorithm uses to decide whether to keep showing your post. A post with high saves outperforms a post with high likes across a 72-hour window, every time.

3 things move the save rate, ranked by impact:

- Embed tool logos inside the infographic. When a reader sees the Clay, Lemlist, Smartlead, HubSpot, or Apollo logo embedded in your framework, they save the post because they want the tool stack later. We started doing this systematically and our save rate roughly doubled.

- Make the framework self-contained. A reader should be able to screenshot the image alone and walk away with the entire playbook. If they need the post body to understand the visual, the save value drops.

- Add one animated element. A small dot tracing a workflow, a line filling progressively, a subtle pulse on the most important box. Animation extends dwell time from two seconds to seven. One per post is plenty.

We've installed this anatomy across 24+ team members posting from their own profiles. The system that wraps around it (cadence, scoring, content ideation, infographic production, repurposing into X threads and the newsletter) is something I broke down in our 8-Step LinkedIn Playbook that added $5M in ARR. That piece is the operational layer. This piece is what every single post inside that operational layer has to look like.

One post engineered correctly won't change your business. A hundred engineered the same way across a team will rebuild your acquisition channel.

> The unit of a LinkedIn engine isn't the impression. It's the post that earned the impression.

Most LinkedIn advice optimizes for output.

Post five times a week, post at 7am, tag a peer, add a poll. None of it matters if the individual post can't survive the half-second scroll. Get the anatomy right at the unit level first. The volume conversation comes after.
