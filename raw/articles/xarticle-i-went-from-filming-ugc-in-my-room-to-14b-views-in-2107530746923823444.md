## Build a content engine that buys you something

Whether you're after installs, web sign-ups, revenue, or mindshare - your content engine MUST buy it. Views alone are never the KPI.

There are three levels to this and almost everyone stops at the first.

Copy what's working. Find an app in your niche doing numbers, rip the format, post your version. This works - I've seen apps hit $20k MRR doing nothing else. It also caps out fast.

Adapt it. Take the mechanism, not the script, and rebuild it around your product. This is where the real money starts.

Propagate it. Take what wins and run it everywhere, across every account, for as long as it has life left.

Most founders can do the first. Some can do the second. Almost nobody does the third, and the third is where a billion views comes from.

Here's how to do all three:

## 1. Start with a viral format, not a problem

The traditional startup school sequence is find a painful problem, validate it by speaking to 100 potential users, build the app, then figure out marketing. 

That sequence is dead.

Your success ceiling is set on day one, and it's set by the distribution potential, not the product.

So find the format and distribution strategy first. Then build the minimum app the format needs.

Where formats actually come from

Three sources, worst to best.

3. Your direct competitors. The obvious place and the most crowded. Fine for your first fortnight, but understand you're jumping into a very red ocean.
2. A completely different niche. Other app categories entirely or even dropshippers, course sellers, or ecom brands. What I love about the latter 3 options is that someone has already done the hard part - figuring out what makes a person stop scrolling - and they've done it on a product with far worse economics than yours. Take the format, apply it where the LTV is better. 
1. Content that isn't selling anything at all. This is the one almost nobody does, and it's where our best format of the year came from.

It didn't come from studying a competitor. It came from me adapting a video I'd found while scrolling. No product, no promo, just a relationship format that was obviously working organically. We rebuilt it around a client's product (unrelated to the relationship niche) and the first video went past 60 million views.

The best formats are hiding in content that isn't trying to sell you anything, because the virality there is truly organic and the ocean is blue. When you adapt it in the right way so that it properly integrates your product, you have a big winner:

Prescribed doomscrolling:

30 minutes a day inside your niche. 30 minutes a day outside it.

Inside your niche teaches you what's working on products like yours. Outside your niche teaches you what culture is doing, which is where your next format comes from before it ever arrives in your category.

It sounds like a joke. It's the highest-leverage hour in my day and it has been for two years.

What makes a format worth picking:

Four things. All four matter.

→ Viral right now. Not "could work". Not "worked 3 years ago". Currently going off.
→ Scalable. You can make 50 variations of it without it going stale. One-hit formats are lottery tickets, and you can't build a system on one.
→ It's survived a fatigue cycle. Look for formats that hit, dipped, and came back. That's the difference between something with real legs and something that was briefly novel. A format that's only ever gone up has never been tested.
→ It can carry your product on screen. Some formats can hold a full demo. Some can only hold a name or a logo. Both are fine. A format that can hold neither isn't a format, it's a waste of time.

Scroll TikTok, not Reels. Reels runs about two weeks behind. By the time a format lands there, any early-mover advantage is gone.

Once you've got the format, the product mostly designs itself. Work backwards from the video. If the format is a reveal, the app has to do the reveal brilliantly and nothing else has to be excellent. Don't build features that don't appear in the format.

The flipside is the mistake that costs founders the most time: some products just don't have viral potential, and no amount of marketing fixes that.

If you've picked the format first, this takes care of itself - the product inherits the virality of the format you built it for. The trap is falling in love with a product idea in a space where nothing goes viral. Go and look. If you see that nobody is making content about that problem and nothing in that niche is doing numbers, you just saved yourself 6 months of wasted time - congrats!

It takes an evening to check. Most founders don't and find out the hard way.

The part you cannot outsource:

Everything upstream of this is getting commoditized. Building is basically free now. Distribution and taste are the moat.

The frontier 10% of viral taste - spotting the format, knowing which one to adapt, writing the hook - isn't being commoditized, and I don't think it ever will be.

Three rules I'm rigid about.

1. Never let AI write your scripts, especially hooks. I've watched people build hook-generation pipelines and even after being fed dozens of real human hooks, the output always sounds slightly 'off'. Hooks are the specific way a 19-year-old talks about a specific problem in a specific scenario. That type of viral taste can't be emulated by an LLM.
2. Never let creators choose the format. Creators drift to whatever is easiest to film and gets the most views, because views are the only number they can see + usually what they get paid for. Prescribe the format. Prescribe the hooks. Let them own the execution, which is the bit they're genuinely better at than you (or at least have more time for).
3. Content strategy is the last thing to delegate. Delegate the filming. Delegate the editing. Delegate the posting. Keep the taste. It's the most scarce skill; I attribute us getting 10x more views than most agencies to us being 10% better at content strategy - it's just that the benefits within that final 10% are truly exponential.

Your viral sense is the compounding asset in all of this. It's the reason I could go from one app in my bedroom to running this across a lot of brands at once. It's the core part that transfers.

## 2. Run your own account before you run anyone else's

UGC means a creator running a dedicated account for your product. It looks like an organic user, it posts like an organic user, and your product is in every video.

This allows you to achieve 2 key things:
→ Lower CPMs: Influencers charge an absolute fortune and paid ad CPMs can be over $10. This high-volume approach allows you to absolutely shatter those economics and achieve CPMs under $0.30.
→ Organic conversion: UGC done right will look like a normal person coming across your product, picking up their phone, and naturally recommending it. An authentic, natural, powerful buying signal to viewers.

Start with one account and one creator. You. You cannot brief someone on formats if you've never created one, and you can't tell a good creator from a bad one if you've never been a bad one yourself.

Then warm it

This is the step almost everyone skips, and it's why most people's first ten videos die at 0 views and then they wrongly conclude their content is bad.

A healthy, warm account is needed for the true organic performance of a video to come through. A cold account will put a ceiling on a video, regardless of content

An account made 2 minutes ago posting a promo video is the most obvious spam signal on the internet. The platform has no idea who you are or whether you're even a person. Tell it before you ask it for anything.

Days 1-4: use the app like a human. 15-30 minutes a day, scrolling your niche, liking, watching things properly. Watch time is the bit that matters - the algorithm learns what you are from what you finish, not what you swipe past. On Instagram, follow small accounts in your niche who are likely to follow back. At Jenni we made creators reach 40 followers before they were allowed to post anything promotional.

Days 4-7: post, but don't sell. Three days of warmup content. Not your format, not your product. Quick to make, high viral potential. This warms the account and tells you whether it's warm at the same time.

These warmup posts are an incredibly strong indicator, especially on IG. Take this account we recently started on a campaign:

The 2 videos on the right are warmups, the 2 on the left are the creator's first 2 promotional vids featuring the product - over 7.5M views on a brand new account

If warmup goes badly, understand that there are 2 different issues at play - zero views and low views are different situations.

Zero views, or single digits, is almost never a content problem. That's the account - unwarmed, flagged, or shadowbanned.

Low views, a few hundred consistently, is a content problem. The account is fine, the platform pushed the content to the FYP, and people scrolled past.

Founders conflate these constantly and rewrite perfectly good formats because of an account issue. Don't let that be you.

Then hire for your ICP, not your niche

When you do start hiring, don't make the mistake almost everyone does. They've got a study app, so they go and find creators who make study content.

Wrong filter. You don't want creators in your niche. You want creators who look like your user.

If you're building a study tool, hire people who look like actual students. Not people who review study apps - people who are visibly, obviously students, filming in their bedroom at 1am before a deadline. If your ICP is moms, hire moms. If it's people in professional jobs, hire someone who looks like they have one.

This helps both virality and views. If someone sees a video with a mismatched ICP, they're more likely to scroll and even if they do stay, are less likely to trust / follow the CTA.

Sometimes the strongest version is the recommender ICP rather than the user - a teacher recommending the study app, one mom recommending it to another. Same principle: the person on screen has to be credible to the person watching.

## 3. Every video needs a purpose, and the purpose has to be your product

Here's the mistake that costs more money than anything else in short-form and UGC.

You put a video out -> it doesn't mention the product so feels SUPER organic -> it gets 1M views = YAYYYYYY!! 

...right?

WRONG.

Your campaign probably has a bonus structure. Let's say it's a $1 CPM - that video cost you $1,000 and brought in exactly $0 of revenue. You LOST $1,000 and would have been better off with a video that got 0 views.

It's why conversion, formats, and content strategy are so key - you MUST be certain that your unit economics work. Bad formats and content will simply light your money on fire.

Ok. So you learn from that incident and pivot to talking head formats. You write a brilliant, talking, storytelling format. The video works - people stay, it goes off, another million views. And somewhere around second 67, the app gets slipped in. It has nothing to do with the thing they stayed for, nothing to do with the story. The viewer came for a story, so will either drop or simply not care about (or notice!) your product.

That video did its job as content and failed entirely as marketing.

The hook and the product have to be the same idea. Not adjacent. The same. Whatever the hook promises, your product has to be the thing that delivers it.

An example construction that works almost every time:

Problem → promised relief → your product is the mechanism.

"Here's how I FINALLY made my IG pics look aesthetic", "STOP deleting your horizontal pics": You keep watching because you want to know the answer. The answer is the app. The product isn't interrupting the video - it is the payoff. Nobody feels sold to because nobody was interrupted.

Run the test on anything before it ships: if you removed the product entirely, would the video still make sense? If yes, the product was decoration and that video will convert at zero.

What this looks like when you get it right:

Back to those three videos from the intro. Here's one of ours that drove 15M+ views at around the same time. This time, try naming the app:

The PURPOSE of the video is the product.

On another of our campaigns, that came out at 350,000 extra downloads (measured, not assumed) in the first four weeks, at CPI of 7 cents. No paid spend behind any of it.

And at Jenni you could watch it in the revenue directly: one single video drove $10,000 in attributed revenue - a DIFFERENT video to the one that drove $4k MRR, that was about $25,000 in lifetime revenue :)

That's what a view is worth when the video was built properly. Most view counts you see advertised are worth nothing at all.

The comment section is the second half of the job:

The first thing a person does after watching something good is open the comments. That means the top comment is doing as much work as your CTA - is it providing social proof, or is it calling your product vibecoded slop?

How we work it:

→ Seed once a video crosses 10-20k views. Before that it's wasted effort. After that, the comment has time to accumulate likes and climb.
→ Principles, not scripts. Lowercase, informal, the odd typo. Scripted comments read scripted and people can smell them instantly. Give whoever is writing them the intent, not the wording.
→ Seed from a personal account, not the campaign one. A comment from an account that clearly isn't the brand reads as a real person. Mix these in with campaign accounts
→ Catch "what app is this?" It's the highest-intent comment on the internet and it's usually sitting there unanswered.

→ Negative comments are a fork in the road. There's a difference between an argumentative comment that's good for engagement and a flat-out piece of negativity.

Be aware of the difference and get rid of the bad ones, ASAP.

## 4. Iterate the winner. Propagate it. Run it like a VC portfolio.

Finding a winner is taste. Everything after it is operations, and that's where almost everyone leaves the views and money on the table.

A winning format can either be a one-off spike or a template that generates millions more views - your action will determine which.

That's one account. Here's what it looks like across a whole campaign:

We posted a video that went past 60 million views. From that single format, inside the next 30 days we cumulatively produced two more individual videos above 20 million, nine above 5 million, and thirty-four above 1 million.

The original did 60 million, but the follow ups did over 100m.

How to propagate a winner:

→ Have managerial control. You should be reviewing all of your creators' drafts and sending them messages every single day. This granular control provides both impetus and direct control over what can go out.
→ Iterate additively. Change one variable at a time, like a proper A/B test. New hook text, a different opening line, a new visual wrapper, a twist at the end. The format stays intact and you understand your results.
→ Understand the central viral mechanism. There's likely a key principle or formula within the video that drives the virality. Work to diagnose it so you can preserve it in all iterations.
→ Understand that one creator hitting isn't a winner. If a format only works for one person, you've found a good creator, not a good format. A real winner repeats across different accounts and different faces. 
→ Don't spam it, even when it's working. Flooding every account with the same thing at once is how you kill a format in a fortnight instead of running it for three months. It also creates a single point of failure for the campaign if all views are through one format. Follow the VC portfolio structure to prevent dependency.

Run the whole thing as a VC portfolio:

50% proven formats. The things with reliable view bases. This is your floor.
25% iterations. Material changes to those proven formats, betting on one variable.
25% moonshots. Genuinely new formats, most of which will die.

Almost everyone runs 100% of the first bucket. It works beautifully right up until the format dies, and then there's nothing behind it and the campaign falls off a cliff. Moonshoots are the pipeline to stop that happening.

The second portfolio rule is about concentration. You should always be working towards no single account driving most of your views. If one account is carrying 50% of a campaign, it's a single point of failure and one shadowban ends your month. Always monitor account health and rotate out underperforming creators.

How this scales without you losing it

The honest answer to how one person filming in a bedroom becomes a thousand accounts:

20% at the front. I set the format, the principles, the direction. What we're making and why.

75% in the middle. Briefing, filming, editing, scheduling, day-to-day creator management. All delegated, and all done better by people who are better at it than me.

5% at the end. My taste pass before it ships.

That first 20% and last 5% are the whole business. I still do both on every single client campaign we run today. Not the interesting ones. Every one.

The 75% is what makes scale possible. The 25% is what makes it work.
