# how to target enterprise companies on linkedin (and book meetings with them)

how to target enterprise companies on linkedin (and book meetings with them)

one enterprise deal is worth more than fifty small ones,

and the person who owns that budget is more reachable on linkedin than a founder at a 20 person startup...

---

## why enterprise is reachable:

a large company adds employees to a linkedin message.

- you message one named person's profile, and the size of their employer does not filter it

- senior people at large firms leave open profiles on, so you reach them free with inmail

- their linkedin inbox holds a handful of messages, while their work email takes hundreds

- the org is fully mapped on linkedin, so every relevant decision maker is VISIBLE

the enterprise scale works in your favor here, since the people and the reporting lines all sit in one place.

a founder at a tiny company reads every cold email personally,

while an enterprise exec has outsourced their email to filters and lives in a linkedin inbox nobody floods.

the size that scares most people off is the exact thing that makes the buyer reachable and the deal worth chasing.

---

## mapping the account before you send:

an enterprise account is a structure, so you map it before you reach anyone.

- identify the division or business unit that owns the problem you solve

- find the function leader inside that unit, not the company-wide c-suite

- trace the reporting line above and below them

- note recent triggers, meaning a reorg, a new hire, an acquisition, or an earnings comment

the map tells you who to reach and in what order.

reaching a director inside the right division beats reaching the group cto who will never see your problem,

so precision matters more here than at a small company.

---

## finding the right person:

a large org has many stakeholders, so you target several rather than one.

- the function owner, meaning the director or vp over the problem you solve

- the executive above them who controls the budget

- a champion lower down who feels the problem daily

- reach more than one, and let the real decision owner surface in the replies

in enterprise no single person says yes alone, so multi-threading keeps you from betting the whole account on one contact.

you qualify hard before you send too,

since a company with ten thousand employees has thousands who are not your buyer.

reaching the wrong department inside a huge company wastes the send and teaches you nothing, so the qualification does real work here.

---

## the message for an enterprise buyer:

an enterprise buyer weighs different things than a small company, so the message shifts.

- speak to scale, risk, and integration, since those drive a large org's decisions

- reference something specific and current about their company or division

- keep the ask small, since a senior person guards their time hardest

- lead with a relevant problem rather than your service category

- match the language to their world, since a term that lands with a startup founder falls flat with a fortune 500 vp

a message that shows you understand a large organization's constraints stands apart from the volume pitches their team deletes daily.

the small ask matters more at this level, since a request for an opinion gets a reply where a request for a 30-minute call gets ignored.

---

## the enterprise sales cycle:

the enterprise cycle runs longer and involves more people, so you run it differently.

- expect several conversations across multiple stakeholders before a meeting

- nurture more than one contact in the account at once

- hold through internal processes you cannot see or control

- keep the value high in every touch, since a committee sees each one

- give a champion internally the material they need to sell you upward

---

## the payoff matches the effort:

a single enterprise client can outweigh an entire book of small ones,

which is why the longer cycle and the multi threading are worth running properly.

it also changes the volume you need,

since a pipeline of a few large accounts worked well beats a flood of small ones worked carelessly.

you win enterprise by treating each account as a project and by staying in front of it long enough for the internal process to run its course.
